\section{Introduction}

Reinforcement learning has produced a diverse family of algorithms that differ sharply in how they trade off sample efficiency, stability, and computational cost, yet these trade-offs are often easier to read off a textbook than to reproduce in practice. In this report we re-implement and empirically compare four widely cited algorithms: DQN \citep{mnih2013playing}, PPO \citep{schulman2017proximal}, SAC \citep{haarnoja2018soft}, and TD3 \citep{fujimoto2018addressing}. They span value-based, on-policy policy-gradient, and off-policy actor-critic methods, and covering both discrete and continuous action spaces. We evaluate them on a set of classic OpenAI Gym control tasks (CartPole, Acrobot, MountainCar, MountainCarContinuous, and Pendulum), using each environment to expose a different aspect of an algorithm's behaviour: short-horizon credit assignment, sparse rewards, and continuous-action exploration. Beyond final-performance numbers averaged over multiple seeds, we treat the report as an implementation diary, documenting the hyperparameters and small design choices that turned out to be decisive, the bugs and failure modes we ran into, and the qualitative differences in compute cost, policy compactness, and off-policy data reuse that emerged once each algorithm had to actually learn something.


\section{Environments}

All five environments used in this report belong to the \emph{Classic Control} suite of the Gymnasium library \citep{towers2024gymnasium}, a maintained fork of OpenAI Gym. They were chosen because they cover a representative range of properties relevant to evaluating RL algorithms: discrete versus continuous action spaces, dense versus sparse rewards, low- versus higher-dimensional state spaces, and stable versus unstable dynamics. The environments are deliberately low-dimensional, making them well suited to comparing algorithms without the cost and variance introduced by larger benchmarks.

\subsection{CartPole-v1}
CartPole-v1 corresponds to the classical pole-balancing problem of Barto, Sutton, and Anderson \citep{barto1983neuronlike}. A pole is attached by an un-actuated joint to a cart that moves along a frictionless track, and the agent must keep the pole upright by pushing the cart left or right.



The observation space is a 4-dimensional continuous vector containing the cart position, cart velocity, pole angle, and pole angular velocity. The action space is discrete with two actions: push left (0) or push right (1). The magnitude of the applied force is fixed, but the effective change in velocity depends on the current pole angle, since the pole's center of gravity shifts the energy required to move the cart.

A reward of $+1$ is granted at every timestep, including the terminal one, so the return equals the episode length. An episode terminates if the pole tilts more than $\pm 12^\circ$ from vertical or if the cart leaves the $(-2.4, 2.4)$ range on the track. Episodes are truncated at 500 steps, which is also the maximum achievable return and the standard "solved" threshold. Initial states are sampled uniformly in $(-0.05, 0.05)$ for every state variable, making the start near-vertical but not exactly so.

CartPole is the easiest environment in this benchmark: rewards are dense, the state space is small, and a near-optimal linear policy exists.

\subsection{MountainCar-v0}
MountainCar-v0, introduced by Moore \citep{moore1990efficient}, places a car at the bottom of a sinusoidal valley. The car must reach a flag on top of the right hill, but its engine is too weak to climb the slope directly; the agent must learn to build momentum by rocking back and forth between the two hills.


The observation space is 2-dimensional and contains the car's horizontal position (in $[-1.2, 0.6]$) and its velocity (in $[-0.07, 0.07]$). The action space is discrete with three actions: accelerate left (0), do nothing (1), or accelerate right (2). The dynamics are deterministic and given by
\begin{align}
v_{t+1} &= v_t + (a_t - 1) \cdot 0.001 - \cos(3 p_t) \cdot 0.0025, \\
p_{t+1} &= p_t + v_{t+1},
\end{align}
where collisions with either end of the track are inelastic (velocity is set to zero).

A reward of $-1$ is given at every timestep, so the agent is implicitly encouraged to reach the goal as quickly as possible. The episode terminates when the car's position is at least $0.5$, and is truncated after 200 steps. The starting position is sampled uniformly in $[-0.6, -0.4]$ with zero initial velocity.

MountainCar is notoriously difficult for naive RL agents because the reward is effectively sparse: a random policy almost never reaches the goal within 200 steps, so the agent must explore effectively before any informative reward signal becomes available. This makes it a useful stress test for exploration strategies.

\subsection{MountainCarContinuous-v0}
MountainCarContinuous-v0 is the continuous-action variant of the Mountain Car problem. The state space and physical setup are identical to MountainCar-v0, but the action space is a continuous interval $[-1, 1]$ representing the directional force applied to the car. The applied force is the action multiplied by a constant power of $0.0015$, and the dynamics become
\begin{align}
v_{t+1} &= v_t + a_t \cdot 0.0015 - 0.0025 \cdot \cos(3 p_t), \\
p_{t+1} &= p_t + v_{t+1}.
\end{align}

The reward structure differs meaningfully from the discrete version. At each timestep the agent receives a penalty of $-0.1 \cdot a_t^2$ to discourage large actions, and a one-off bonus of $+100$ is added when the car reaches the goal position ($\geq 0.45$). Episodes are truncated after 999 steps. The starting position is uniform in $[-0.6, -0.4]$ and the initial velocity is zero.

This environment shares the exploration challenge of its discrete counterpart but compounds it with a credit-assignment problem: aggressive action use is locally penalized, yet such actions are necessary to escape the valley. It is the only environment in this study where exploration must be performed in a continuous action space.

\subsection{Pendulum-v1}
Pendulum-v1 is the classical inverted pendulum swing-up problem. A pendulum attached at one end to a fixed pivot starts in a random orientation, and the agent must apply torque at the joint to bring it to and hold it in the upright position.




The observation space is 3-dimensional and contains $\cos(\theta)$, $\sin(\theta)$, and the angular velocity $\dot{\theta} \in [-8, 8]$, where $\theta$ is the pendulum's angle. Encoding the angle through its sine and cosine avoids discontinuities at $\pm\pi$. The action space is a continuous torque in $[-2, 2]$ N·m, positive counter-clockwise.

The reward at each step is
\[
r_t = -\left(\theta_t^2 + 0.1\,\dot{\theta}_t^2 + 0.001\,\tau_t^2\right),
\]
with $\theta$ normalized to $[-\pi, \pi]$ (zero being upright). The maximum per-step reward is $0$ (upright, motionless, no torque) and the minimum is approximately $-16.27$. Episodes have no termination condition and are always truncated at 200 steps. The initial state samples $\theta$ uniformly from $[-\pi, \pi]$ and $\dot{\theta}$ from $[-1, 1]$.

Unlike Mountain Car, the reward is dense and informative everywhere in the state space. The challenge is the underactuated dynamics: the maximum torque is insufficient to lift the pendulum directly, so the agent must learn to pump energy through repeated swings before stabilizing at the top.

\subsection{Acrobot-v1}
Acrobot-v1, based on Sutton's formulation \citep{sutton1996generalization}, consists of two links connected linearly to form a chain. The upper end of the chain is fixed and the joint between the two links is actuated. Starting from a downward-hanging position, the agent must swing the free tip above a target height, equivalent to the length of one link above the pivot.

The observation space is 6-dimensional and contains $\cos(\theta_1)$, $\sin(\theta_1)$, $\cos(\theta_2)$, $\sin(\theta_2)$, and the two angular velocities $\dot{\theta}_1 \in [-4\pi, 4\pi]$ and $\dot{\theta}_2 \in [-9\pi, 9\pi]$. Here $\theta_1$ is the angle of the first link from vertical-down and $\theta_2$ is the angle of the second link relative to the first. The action space is discrete with three values, corresponding to applying torque of $-1$, $0$, or $+1$ N·m at the actuated joint.

A reward of $-1$ is given at each step until the goal is reached, at which point the episode terminates with a final reward of $0$. The termination condition is $-\cos(\theta_1) - \cos(\theta_1 + \theta_2) > 1.0$. Episodes are truncated at 500 steps, and the reward threshold for "solving" the task is $-100$. Each component of the underlying state is initialized uniformly in $[-0.1, 0.1]$, so the system starts essentially hanging straight down with small perturbations.

Acrobot combines the underactuated swing-up challenge of Pendulum with the higher-dimensional state space and discrete actions of CartPole, making it a useful intermediate test case in this benchmark.

\section{Algorithms}
\subsection{Deep Q-Learning (DQN)}
\label{sec:dqn}
Deep Q-Learning (DQN) is a value-based, off-policy algorithm for discrete action spaces which was introduced by researchers at DeepMind in 2013 \citep{mnih2013playing} and further developed two years later \citep{mnih2015human} as a variant of Q-learning \citep{watkins1992q} based on deep neural networks. Although DQN was not the first algorithm to implement neural networks as function approximators in a reinforcement learning setting, it was the first one to do so in an efficient, end-to-end manner, solving most of the shortcomings of previous approaches like Tesauro's TD-gammon \citep{tesauro1995temporal}. 

The main issues DQN addressed were sample correlation and the non-stationarity of the underlying distribution, which both arise from the fact that the data samples in a reinforcement learning setting are inherently correlated and the data distribution is subject to change while training the algorithm (as opposed to supervised learning where the data samples are assumed to be independent and coming from the same, fixed distribution). The algorithm circumvents these problems by implementing an experience replay buffer \citep{lin1992reinforcement} which allows the model to sample mini-batches of previous transitions randomly during the training procedure, thus eliminating the sample correlation and smoothing the data distribution over past experiences.

For the project we purposefully based our implementation on the original algorithm (outlined in Algorithm \ref{alg:dqn}) as implemented in \citep{mnih2013playing}, without the addition of a target network (put forward in \citep{mnih2015human}) in order to draw sharper contrasts with the other neural network based algorithms examined in this paper (namely TD3 and SAC). The only significant modification we made to the algorithm was the substitution of a convolutional neural network architecture for a multilayer perceptron one since our test environments returned states as numerical values as opposed to images like in the original Atari setting.

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{Deep Q-learning with Experience Replay (based on \citep{mnih2013playing})}
\label{alg:dqn}
\begin{algorithmic}
\State Initialize replay memory $D$ to capacity $N$
\State Initialize action-value function $Q$ with random weights $\theta$
\For{episode $= 1, M$}
    \State Initialize starting state $s_1$ from the environment
    \For{$t = 1, T$}
        \State With probability $\epsilon$ select a random action $a_t$
        \State otherwise select $a_t = \arg\max_a Q(s_t, a; \theta)$
        \State Execute action $a_t$ in emulator and observe reward $r_t$ and next state $s_{t+1}$
        \State Store transition $(s_t, a_t, r_t, s_{t+1})$ in $D$
        \State Sample random minibatch of transitions $(s_j, a_j, r_j, s_{j+1})$ from $D$
        \State Set $y_j = 
        \begin{cases} 
        r_j & \text{for terminal } s_{j+1}\\
        r_j + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s_{j+1}, a'; \theta) & \text{for non-terminal } s_{j+1}
        \end{cases}$
        \State Perform a gradient descent step on $(y_j - Q(s_j, a_j\theta))^2$
    \EndFor
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Twin Delayed Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (TD3)}
\label{sec:td3}
Twin Delayed Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient is an off-policy actor-critic algorithm \citep{fujimoto2018addressing} for continuous action spaces which serves as an upgrade to the then state of the art actor-critic method Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (DDPG) \citep{lillicrap2019continuouscontroldeepreinforcement}, building upon it while addressing its main shortcomings. The main problem TD3 addresses is overestimation bias which is a well documented property of Q-learning in discrete settings \citep{thrun2014issues} but also present in actor-critic methods, arising from the repeated maximization of noisy value estimates resulting in constant overestimation which is exacerbated by the bootstrapping nature of temporal difference learning. 

TD3 aims to reduce the inherent overestimation bias by the introduction of "clipped double Q-learning" which entails the usage of two independent critic networks which are used in the Bellman update by taking the minimum of their output, thus binding the estimate for the value of the future state when updating the target (line 9, Algorithm \ref{alg:td3}). In addition to the implementation of the twin critics TD3 reduces the function approximation error by the application of target networks \citep{mnih2015human} and the novel idea of delayed policy updates which means updating the actor less frequently than the critics resulting in lower variance value updates and thus higher quality policy updates (line 10-13, in Algorithm \ref{alg:td3}). Last but not least TD3 tackles the problem inherent in DDPG and all methods equipped with deterministic policies, namely their tendency to overfit to narrow peaks in the value estimate, which is solved by a form of regularisation called target policy smoothing. This works similarly to the learning update used in SARSA \citep{sutton1998reinforcement}, by adding a tiny bit of random noise to the action selected by the target policy before showing it to the critic (line 8, Algorithm \ref{alg:td3}). The method reinforces the idea that similar actions should have similar values, by enforcing the critic to evaluate a small area around the target action instead of fixating on "narrow spikes", thus smootning the value estimate.

In our implementation of TD3 we stuck to the algorithm outlined in \citep{fujimoto2018addressing} as shown in Algorithm \ref{alg:td3}.

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{Twin Delayed Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient \citep{fujimoto2018addressing}}
\label{alg:td3}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State Initialize critic networks $Q_{\theta_1}$, $Q_{\theta_2}$, and actor network $\pi_\phi$ with random parameters $\theta_1, \theta_2, \phi$
\State Initialize target networks $\theta'_1 \gets \theta_1, \theta'_2 \gets \theta_2, \phi' \gets \phi$
\State Initialize replay buffer $\mathcal{B}$
\For{$t = 1$ \textbf{to} $T$}
    \State Select action with exploration noise $a \sim \pi_\phi(s) + \epsilon$, where $\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma)$, and observe reward $r$ and new state $s'$
    \State Store transition tuple $(s, a, r, s')$ in $\mathcal{B}$
    \State Sample mini-batch of $N$ transitions $(s, a, r, s')$ from $\mathcal{B}$
    \State $\tilde{a} \gets \pi_{\phi'}(s') + \epsilon$, where $\epsilon \sim \text{clip}(\mathcal{N}(0, \tilde{\sigma}), -c, c)$
    \State $y \gets r + \gamma \min_{i=1,2} Q_{\theta'_i}(s', \tilde{a})$
    \State Update critics $\theta_i \gets \arg\min_{\theta_i} N^{-1} \sum \left( y - Q_{\theta_i}(s, a) \right)^2$
    \If{$t \bmod d = 0$}
        \State Update $\phi$ by the deterministic policy gradient:
        \Statex \qquad $\nabla_\phi J(\phi) = N^{-1} \sum \nabla_a Q_{\theta_1}(s, a) \big|_{a=\pi_\phi(s)} \nabla_\phi \pi_\phi(s)$
        \State Update target networks:
        \Statex \qquad $\theta'_i \gets \tau \theta_i + (1 - \tau)\theta'_i$
        \Statex \qquad $\phi' \gets \tau \phi + (1 - \tau)\phi'$
    \EndIf
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Soft Actor-Critic (SAC)}
\label{sec:sac}

Soft Actor-Critic \citep{haarnoja2018soft} is an off-policy actor-critic algorithm for continuous action spaces. Like TD3 (Section~\ref{sec:td3}) it is trained off-policy from an experience replay buffer (Section~\ref{sec:dqn}) and uses clipped double-Q learning to curb the overestimation bias described there. Its distinctive feature is that it optimises the \emph{maximum-entropy} objective, which augments the expected return with the expected policy entropy:
\begin{equation}
    J(\pi) \;=\; \sum_{t=0}^{T} \mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{s}_t,\mathbf{a}_t)\sim \rho_\pi}
    \Big[\, r(\mathbf{s}_t,\mathbf{a}_t) \;+\; \alpha\,\mathcal{H}\!\left(\pi(\cdot\,|\,\mathbf{s}_t)\right) \,\Big],
    \label{eq:sac-objective}
\end{equation}
where the temperature $\alpha \ge 0$ trades off reward against entropy and recovers the standard RL objective as $\alpha \to 0$. Unlike the fixed additive action noise of TD3/DDPG, this entropy bonus gives SAC a \emph{built-in, state-dependent exploration mechanism}, spreading probability mass across near-optimal actions and yielding policies that are robust to modelling errors \citep{ziebart2010thesis,haarnoja2017energy}.

\paragraph{Function approximators and updates.}
SAC approximates the corresponding maximum-entropy policy iteration \citep{haarnoja2018soft} with a stochastic policy $\pi_\phi$ and a soft Q-function (here also a soft value $V_\psi$). The policy is a Gaussian whose mean and log-std are outputs of an MLP, with a $\tanh$ squashing that bounds the sampled action to the valid range and a matching change-of-variables correction to its log-probability. All networks are trained on minibatches $(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{a},r,\mathbf{s}')\sim\mathcal{D}$: the Q-function minimises a soft Bellman residual whose bootstrap target adds an entropy term to the next-state value and, as in TD3, takes $\min_{i=1,2} Q_{\theta_i}$ over twin critics to limit overestimation; the target value network is maintained as a Polyak-averaged copy (Section~\ref{sec:td3}). The policy minimises the expected KL divergence to the exponential of the Q-function; using the reparameterisation $\mathbf{a} = f_\phi(\epsilon;\mathbf{s})$ with $\epsilon$ drawn from a fixed noise distribution, this becomes the low-variance objective
\begin{equation}
    J_\pi(\phi) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{s}\sim\mathcal{D},\,\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}}
    \!\left[\log \pi_\phi\!\left(f_\phi(\epsilon;\mathbf{s})\,|\,\mathbf{s}\right) -
        Q_\theta\!\left(\mathbf{s},\,f_\phi(\epsilon;\mathbf{s})\right)\right].
    \label{eq:sac-pi-loss}
\end{equation}
The full update (Algorithm~\ref{alg:sac}) alternates one environment step under the current stochastic policy with gradient steps on these losses and a Polyak update of the target network. Because the critic updates need only $(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{a},r,\mathbf{s}')$ tuples, SAC is fully off-policy and reuses the entire buffer; the only on-policy quantities are the freshly-sampled actions used to form the value- and policy-loss targets.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Soft Actor-Critic \citep{haarnoja2018soft}}
\label{alg:sac}
\begin{algorithmic}
\State Initialise parameters $\psi, \bar\psi, \theta_1, \theta_2, \phi$ and replay buffer $\mathcal{D} \gets \emptyset$.
\For{each iteration}
    \For{each environment step}
        \State $\mathbf{a}_t \sim \pi_\phi(\cdot\,|\,\mathbf{s}_t)$;\;
               $\mathbf{s}_{t+1} \sim p(\cdot\,|\,\mathbf{s}_t,\mathbf{a}_t)$
        \State $\mathcal{D} \gets \mathcal{D} \cup \{(\mathbf{s}_t,\mathbf{a}_t,r_t,\mathbf{s}_{t+1})\}$
    \EndFor
    \For{each gradient step}
        \State $\psi \gets \psi - \lambda_V \hat\nabla_\psi J_V(\psi)$
        \State $\theta_i \gets \theta_i - \lambda_Q \hat\nabla_{\theta_i} J_Q(\theta_i)\;\;\text{for } i \in \{1,2\}$
        \State $\phi \gets \phi - \lambda_\pi \hat\nabla_\phi J_\pi(\phi)$
        \State $\bar\psi \gets \tau \psi + (1-\tau)\bar\psi$
    \EndFor
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsubsection{Hyperparameter Sensitivity}

We ran 143-trial Optuna sweeps of SAC on the two continuous-control environments in our benchmark. The swept set is identical across environments except that MountainCarContinuous-v0 also varies \texttt{warmup\_action\_repeat}, the knob from Section~\ref{sec:sac} used to seed the replay buffer with successful trajectories. Trials use the same training budget and three evaluation seeds as our final runs, and we report the mean undiscounted episodic return of the final policy.

\paragraph{MountainCarContinuous-v0.}
On this sparse-reward task only about 21 percent of trials cross our ``solved'' threshold of return $\geq 80$; the rest are stuck near zero. Three knobs separate the two regimes. First, \texttt{warmup\_action\_repeat} must sit in a narrow band: a value of 1 solves 0 of 29 trials, values of 5 or 10 solve roughly 28 percent each, and a value of 20 over-commits to a single direction and drops the success rate back to 10 percent. Second, the discount factor must lie in $\{0.999, 0.9999\}$, which solve 32 and 27 percent of trials respectively; the other settings we tested (0.99, 0.995, 0.9995, 0.99995) all collapse to a 0 percent solve rate. Third, a batch size of 128 beats 256 and 512 by a wide margin (28 percent versus 0 and 11), plausibly because smaller minibatches expose the buffer's rare goal-reaching transitions more often per gradient step. Outside of those three knobs SAC is essentially insensitive to the temperature $\alpha$, the learning rate, and the Polyak rate $\tau$ across the ranges swept. Once the buffer contains successful trajectories the 2-dimensional state space is easy enough that fine-grained optimizer choices stop mattering.

\paragraph{Pendulum-v1.}
With dense rewards the picture flips. About 48 percent of trials reach a loose ``good'' threshold of return $\geq -200$, and the sensitivity is concentrated on the optimization side rather than on exploration. Learning rate is the dominant knob: the lowest swept quartile (below roughly $3 \times 10^{-4}$) never crosses the threshold, while the upper three quartiles solve between 56 and 72 percent of trials. Counter-intuitively, lower discount factors work better here: the bottom quartile of the swept range (0.95 to 0.968) solves 66 percent of trials, the top quartile (0.983 to 0.999) only 31 percent. Pendulum episodes are 200 truncated steps with no real long-horizon credit, so larger $\gamma$ mostly inflates variance in the bootstrap target. The Polyak rate peaks in a narrow band of $\tau \in [0.004, 0.007]$ and degrades at either end; batch size 128 again beats 256 and 512; and a hidden dim of 128 beats both 64 (too small) and 256 (overparameterised for this short-horizon task). The entropy temperature $\alpha$ barely moves the result over two orders of magnitude, consistent with the SAC paper's claim that the method is robust to $\alpha$ once it is in a sensible range.

\paragraph{Takeaway.}
The hyperparameters that drive SAC's performance are environment dependent rather than universal. Exploration-side knobs (\texttt{warmup\_action\_repeat} and a careful $\gamma$) dominate on sparse-reward MountainCarContinuous, while optimization-side knobs ($\eta$, $\gamma$, $\tau$) dominate on dense-reward Pendulum. The entropy temperature $\alpha$ has only a small effect on either environment, which lines up with $\alpha$ being precisely the parameter SAC's automatic-temperature variant is designed to remove from the tuning loop.

\subsection{Proximal policy optimization (PPO)}
\subsubsection{Baseline Algorithm}
Proximal Policy Optimization \citep{schulman2017proximal} is an on-policy actor-critic algorithm for continuous and discrete action spaces. It addresses a core difficulty in policy gradient methods: taking too large an update step can collapse performance, yet constraining updates via explicit KL penalties (as in TRPO \citep{....}) is computationally expensive. PPO-Clip resolves this with a simple clipped surrogate objective that implicitly enforces a trust region, making it both stable and easy to implement.

\paragraph{Maximum-entropy objective and policy ratio:} Let $\pi_{\theta_k}$ denote the behaviour policy that collected rollout $\mathcal{D}_k$. PPO measures how much the updated policy $\pi_\theta$ deviates from $\pi_{\theta_k}$ via the probability ratio
\begin{equation}
    r_t(\theta) \;=\; \frac{\pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)}{\pi_{\theta_k}(a_t \mid s_t)},
\end{equation}
which equals one when $\theta = \theta_k$ and drifts away as the policy changes. The clipped surrogate objective is
\begin{equation}
    L^{\mathrm{CLIP}}(\theta)
    \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{t \sim \mathcal{D}_k}
    \!\left[
        \min\!\left(
            r_t(\theta)\,\hat{A}_t,\;
            \mathrm{clip}\!\left(r_t(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon\right)\hat{A}_t
        \right)
    \right],
    \label{eq:ppo-clip}
\end{equation}
where $\varepsilon$ (typically $0.2$) controls how far the ratio is allowed to move. The $\min$ makes the objective a lower bound: when $\hat{A}_t > 0$, the update is prevented from becoming too optimistic by capping $r_t$ at $1+\varepsilon$; when $\hat{A}_t < 0$, the ratio is floored at $1-\varepsilon$. This achieves a trust-region effect without solving a constrained optimisation problem.

\paragraph{Generalized Advantage Estimation:} Advantages $\hat{A}_t$ are estimated via GAE-$\lambda$ \citep{...}, which balances bias and variance through a weighted sum of TD residuals. For $\delta_t = r_t + \gamma V(s_{t+1})(1-d_t) - V(s_t)$, where $d_t$ is a terminal flag,
\begin{equation}
    \hat{A}_t \;=\; \sum_{l=0}^{T-t-1} (\gamma\lambda)^l\,\delta_{t+l},
    \label{eq:gae}
\end{equation}
computed backward over the rollout of horizon $T$. The corresponding return target for the value function is $\hat{R}_t = \hat{A}_t + V_{\theta_k}(s_t)$. Setting $\lambda = 1$ recovers the full Monte Carlo return (high variance, low bias), while $\lambda = 0$ reduces to a one-step TD target (low variance, higher bias); intermediate values trade off these extremes.

\paragraph{Combined loss:} The actor and critic share a single neural network (separate output heads), so all parameters are updated jointly. Each stochastic gradient step minimises
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}(\theta)
    \;=\; -L^{\mathrm{CLIP}}(\theta)
    \;+\; c_v\,\mathbb{E}_t\!\left[\left(V_\theta(s_t) - \hat{R}_t\right)^2\right]
    \;-\; c_e\,\mathbb{E}_t\!\left[\mathcal{H}\!\left(\pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s_t)\right)\right],
    \label{eq:ppo-loss}
\end{equation}
where $c_v$ and $c_e$ weight the value loss and an entropy bonus, respectively. The entropy term encourages exploration by penalising premature collapse of the action distribution.

% \paragraph{Algorithm:} PPO alternates between collecting on-policy rollouts and performing multiple epochs of minibatch gradient descent on the same data (Algorithm~\ref{alg:ppo}). Unlike off-policy methods, the rollout buffer is discarded after each update cycle, so all data used for training was generated by the current policy. To keep the probability ratios $r_t(\theta)$ well-defined, the log-probabilities $\log\pi_{\theta_k}(a_t \mid s_t)$ are stored at collection time and held fixed during the $K$ update epochs. Advantages are normalised to zero mean and unit variance within each minibatch to stabilise training, and gradient norms are clipped to prevent excessively large update steps.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Proximal Policy Optimization -- PPO-Clip \citep{schulman2017proximal}}
\label{alg:ppo}
\begin{algorithmic}
\State Initialise actor-critic parameters $\theta$ and empty rollout buffer $\mathcal{B}$.
\While{total environment steps not reached}
    \For{each environment step (up to horizon $T$)}
        \State Sample $a_t \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s_t)$;\; store $(s_t, a_t, r_t, d_t, \log\pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t))$ in $\mathcal{B}$
    \EndFor
    \State Bootstrap $V_\theta(s_T)$;\; compute $\hat{A}_t$ and $\hat{R}_t$ via GAE-$\lambda$ (backward pass over $\mathcal{B}$)
    \For{$K$ epochs over $\mathcal{B}$}
        \For{each minibatch of size $M$}
            \State Normalise $\hat{A}_t$ to zero mean and unit variance on the minibatch
            \State Compute $r_t(\theta) = \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)\,/\,\pi_{\theta_k}(a_t \mid s_t)$
            \State Evaluate $L^{\mathrm{CLIP}}$, value loss, and entropy bonus (Eq.~\ref{eq:ppo-loss})
            \State $\theta \gets \theta - \alpha\,\hat\nabla_\theta\,\mathcal{L}(\theta)$;\; clip $\|\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}\|_2$ at max norm
        \EndFor
    \EndFor
    \State Discard $\mathcal{B}$ (on-policy: data is no longer valid after $\theta$ has changed)
\EndWhile
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsubsection{Hyperparameter Optimization}
\label{sec:ppo-hpo}

We study PPO's sensitivity to hyperparameters using three complementary approaches: (i) one-factor-at-a-time (OFAT) sweeps to isolate individual effects, (ii) random search as a model-free baseline, and (iii) Optuna TPE~\citep{akiba2019optuna} as a Bayesian alternative. All methods use the same evaluation protocol: after training for a fixed budget, we freeze the policy and compute the mean undiscounted return over 10 deterministic episodes. This \emph{evaluation return} serves as the trial score for ranking configurations.

\paragraph{Search space.}
Table~\ref{tab:ppo-search-space} defines the joint search space used for random and Optuna searches. We use a conservative space for easy environments (CartPole, Acrobot) and an extended space including $\gamma$, $n_{\mathrm{steps}}$, and network size for harder tasks (MountainCar, Pendulum).

\paragraph{Methods.}
\textbf{OFAT} varies one hyperparameter over a grid while holding others at the paper default~\citep{schulman2017proximal}, averaged over 3 seeds. This isolates individual sensitivities but ignores interactions.
\textbf{Random search} draws $N$ independent configurations from the joint space---a strong baseline that explores the full space without modelling~\citep{bergstra2012random}.
\textbf{Optuna TPE} (Tree-structured Parzen Estimator) models the objective as a function of hyperparameters and proposes trials expected to improve over past observations~\citep{bergstra2011algorithms}. Algorithm~\ref{alg:hpo} summarises the search loop; the key difference is how $\mathbf{h}$ is sampled (uniformly vs.\ from the TPE surrogate).

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Hyperparameter search (Random / Optuna)}
\label{alg:hpo}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Search space $\mathcal{H}$, number of trials $N$, training budget $T$, eval episodes $E$
\State $\mathcal{R} \gets \emptyset$ \Comment{Results}
\For{$i = 1, \ldots, N$}
    \State $\mathbf{h} \gets \textsc{Sample}(\mathcal{H}, \mathcal{R})$ \Comment{Random: uniform; TPE: surrogate model}
    \State $\pi \gets \textsc{TrainPPO}(\mathbf{h}, T)$ \Comment{Train for $T$ env steps}
    \State $s \gets \frac{1}{E}\sum_{j=1}^{E} G_j(\pi)$ \Comment{Mean return over $E$ deterministic episodes}
    \State $\mathcal{R} \gets \mathcal{R} \cup \{(\mathbf{h}, s)\}$
\EndFor
\State \Return $\arg\max_{(\mathbf{h},s)\in\mathcal{R}} s$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Soft Actor-Critic (SAC)}
SAC's stochastic actor and entropy bonus give it a built-in exploration mechanism, in contrast to the additive action noise used by TD3 and DDPG, and its off-policy formulation makes it considerably more sample-efficient than PPO. The cost is a higher per-iteration compute footprint: each update touches four networks ($V_\psi$, $V_{\bar\psi}$, $Q_{\theta_1}$, $Q_{\theta_2}$) plus the actor, and requires a fresh policy sample for the value and policy losses. These properties play out cleanly on Pendulum-v1, where SAC attains the best mean return of any algorithm in our benchmark, but they were not enough to solve MountainCarContinuous-v0 out of the box.

\paragraph{Vanilla SAC stalls at zero return on MountainCarContinuous.}
With the default hyperparameters from \citet{haarnoja2018soft}, our implementation never escaped a trivial ``do nothing'' policy on MountainCarContinuous, plateauing at an evaluation return of approximately zero. Two compounding issues, neither obvious from the paper, were responsible.

First, the uniform-random warmup phase essentially never reaches the goal. Per-step iid actions in $[-1, 1]$ produce a velocity standard deviation of $\sigma_v \approx 0.028$, well below the $\sim 0.07$ peak velocity required to climb out of the valley. Empirically, across 25 warmup episodes the goal was reached zero times and the maximum position attained was $-0.21$ (the goal is at $+0.45$). Because the replay buffer never sees a positive reward, the policy correctly identifies that the action-energy penalty $-0.1\,a_t^2$ is minimised by inaction and converges there.

Second, the standard discount $\gamma = 0.99$ effectively hides the $+100$ terminal bonus. With $\sim 500$ steps required to reach the goal from the start, the discounted goal value is $0.99^{500} \cdot 100 \approx 0.66$, comparable to the cumulative action-cost penalty. Even when goal-reaching transitions enter the buffer, the bootstrapped target underweights them relative to the per-step cost.

\paragraph{Two hyperparameter changes are sufficient.}
Both issues can be addressed without modifying the algorithm. We hold each warmup action for ten consecutive environment steps (\texttt{warmup\_action\_repeat}$=10$), which lets random actions accumulate enough momentum to actually escape the valley; this raises the per-episode goal-reach rate during warmup from $0\%$ to roughly $70\%$, so the buffer is seeded with informative transitions. We then raise the discount to $\gamma = 0.9999$, restoring the discounted goal value to $\approx 95$ and ensuring that the terminal bonus dominates the integrated action penalty.






\subsection{Proximal policy optimization (PPO)}

\paragraph{One-factor-at-a-time (OFAT):}
OFAT sweeps reveal that PPO's sensitivity is environment-dependent.
On \textbf{CartPole-v1} (Figure~\ref{fig:cartpole_ppo_hyperparameters}, Appendix), learning rate has minimal impact once above $10^{-4}$, and all tested $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{clip}}$ values converge to near-optimal return; the task is simple enough that most reasonable settings succeed.
On \textbf{Acrobot-v1}, moderate learning rates ($3{\times}10^{-4}$) outperform both lower and higher values, and non-zero entropy ($c_e \approx 0.01$) accelerates early exploration without harming final performance.
\textbf{MountainCar-v0} is the most sensitive: only $c_e \geq 0.01$ escapes the local minimum at $-200$; $\lambda_{\mathrm{GAE}} = 1.0$ (Monte Carlo returns) outperforms lower values; and $\gamma \geq 0.99$ is required for adequate credit assignment over the long horizon.
On \textbf{Pendulum-v1}, higher learning rates ($\geq 10^{-3}$) learn faster, and $\lambda_{\mathrm{GAE}} \in [0.9, 0.95]$ balances bias and variance effectively.

\paragraph{Joint search: random vs.\ Optuna:}
Table~\ref{tab:ppo-joint-search} summarises joint-search results on Pendulum-v1 and MountainCar-v0 (10 trials each, seed 0, $10^5$ steps).
On \textbf{Pendulum}, random search found a configuration scoring $-144.6$, substantially better than Optuna's best ($-954.8$). This counter-intuitive result arises because (i) the trial budget is small relative to the 9-dimensional space, limiting TPE's ability to build an accurate surrogate, and (ii) random search uniformly covers the space, occasionally sampling high-quality regions that TPE's early model underweights.
On \textbf{MountainCar}, only 1 of 10 random trials escaped the $-200$ failure mode, achieving $-140.4$; the remaining 9 trials failed to reach the goal within the training budget. This highlights MountainCar's sparse-reward challenge: without sufficient entropy or the right $\gamma$, PPO converges to a degenerate policy.

\paragraph{Optuna importance analysis:}
Figure~\ref{fig:importances} shows fANOVA-based hyperparameter importances from the Optuna study on Pendulum-v1. Network capacity (\texttt{hidden\_sizes}), training intensity ($n_{\mathrm{epochs}}$, batch size), and temporal parameters ($\gamma$, $\mathrm{lr}$, $\lambda_{\mathrm{GAE}}$) each contribute 13--19\% of outcome variance. In contrast, $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{clip}}$ and $c_e$ contribute only 2\% each, consistent with PPO's design goal of robustness to the clipping threshold once it lies in a sensible range.

\paragraph{Learning dynamics:}
Figure~\ref{fig:best-random-vs-optuna} compares training episode returns for the best random and Optuna trials on Pendulum. The random-search winner (trial 5) shows steady improvement throughout training, while Optuna's best (trial 9) plateaus earlier at a worse level. Figures~\ref{fig:random-eval} and~\ref{fig:optuna-top3} (Appendix) plot the \emph{evaluation} metric used for scoring: 10 deterministic episodes at each checkpoint. The final dashed line ($-144.6$ vs.\ $-954.8$) is the trial score reported in Table~\ref{tab:ppo-joint-search}.

\paragraph{Best configurations:}
Table~\ref{tab:pendulum-best-hparams} lists the winning hyperparameters for Pendulum. Both methods selected $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{clip}}=0.1$, $n_{\mathrm{epochs}}=5$, $n_{\mathrm{steps}}=4096$, and $(128,128)$ hidden layers. The key difference is $\gamma$: random's best uses $\gamma=0.9$, while Optuna chose $\gamma=0.995$. Pendulum's 200-step episodes with dense rewards benefit from lower discount factors that reduce variance in the value bootstrap---a finding consistent with our SAC analysis on the same environment.

\paragraph{Limitations:}
Our HPO experiments use a single training seed and 10 evaluation episodes per trial, introducing non-negligible variance in trial rankings. Large performance gaps (e.g., $-144$ vs.\ $-955$) are unlikely to reverse, but close trials may swap order under resampling. Future work should increase seed count and trial budget for more robust conclusions.
